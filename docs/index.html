Lab 0: Prerequisites, Physical Architecture & Network Setup

Setting up Baxter from zero: mechanical mounting, AC power, link-local networking, and host connectivity.

1. Objectives

By the end of this lab, you will: 1. Understand Baxter's physical safety architecture, component layout, and electrical specifications. 2. 2. Hardware Architecture & Components

Baxter consists of the following key subsystems:

  1. Torso & Pedestal: Houses the master computer (Intel Core i7 x86), power supplies, motor bus relays, rear power switch, and rear I/O connectors.
  2. Dual 7-DOF Articulated Limbs: Driven by Series Elastic Actuators (SEAs) with integrated mechanical springs and joint torque sensing.
  3. Neck & Head Assembly: Contains a 1024x600 color LCD display, head pan motor ($\pm 90^\circ$), head nod solenoid, 360° halo LED indicator, and 12-sensor ultrasonic sonar ring.
  4. End-Effectors & Cuffs: Two quick-disconnect tool mounting plates, cuff navigators (rotary dial + two buttons), and cuff grip squeeze sensors (Zero-G mode).
  5. Integrated Cameras: Three 1.2 megapixel CMOS sensors (Head, Left Wrist, Right Wrist).

3. Power-On Sequence

  1. Safety Clearance: Verify that Baxter's dual arms have at least 1.5 meters of unobstructed clearance in all directions.
  2. AC Line Power: Plug the standard IEC 3-prong power cord into a grounded 120V/240V AC wall receptacle.
  3. Main Power Switch: Toggle the master rocker switch located at the rear bottom of Baxter's waist to the ON (I) position.
  4. Boot Sequence: - The cooling fans will spin up to full speed and settle to an idle whisper. - The torso LED halo will illuminate yellow. - The internal x86 computer boots Ubuntu Linux (takes ~60 to 90 seconds). - Once the onboard software boots, the head LCD screen displays Baxter's sleeping or blank face, and the halo turns green or stays yellow (waiting for safety interlock).

4. Workstation Network Configuration

Baxter communicates over standard Ethernet using a direct cable or a local Gigabit switch.

Option A: Direct Peer-to-Peer Link-Local (Recommended)

Connect an Ethernet cable directly from your laptop or workstation into the RJ45 port on Baxter's rear waist.

  1. Inspect Your Ethernet Interface Name: bash ip -br addr # Example output: enx00e04c2e5460 or eth0

  2. Configure Link-Local (Automatic IPv4): Baxter automatically assigns itself an IPv4 link-local address in the 169.254.X.X / 16 subnet (e.g. 169.254.9.243). Configure your workstation's Ethernet interface: bash # Using NetworkManager CLI (nmcli) nmcli con add type ethernet con-name baxter-link ifname <your_interface> ipv4.method link-local nmcli con up baxter-link Or manually assign a static address within the same link-local subnet: bash sudo ip addr add 169.254.254.8/16 dev <your_interface> sudo ip link set <your_interface> up

  3. Verify Connectivity: Discover Baxter on the network using arp-scan or avahi-browse: bash # Scan the subnet for Baxter sudo arp-scan --interface=<your_interface> 169.254.0.0/16 Ping Baxter (replace with Baxter's IP address or hostname <serial_number>.local): bash ping -c 3 169.254.9.243 Expected Output: text 64 bytes from 169.254.9.243: icmp_seq=1 ttl=64 time=0.452 ms 64 bytes from 169.254.9.243: icmp_seq=2 ttl=64 time=0.388 ms 3 packets transmitted, 3 received, 0% packet loss

  4. Verify Rosbridge WebSocket Port (9090): Baxter runs a Rosbridge WebSocket server that exposes the entire ROS master API over WebSockets: bash nc -zv 169.254.9.243 9090 # Output: Connection to 169.254.9.243 9090 port [tcp/*] succeeded!

2. The Symptom: E-Stop is Cleared, But Robot Still Refuses to Enable

With a reviewed, functioning emergency-stop system in place, a robot can still report a joint-limit fault:

$ baxter status
Robot Status:
  enabled: False
  stopped: False       <-- E-stop is clear
  error: True          <-- But error is still True!
  estop_button: 0
  estop_source: 0

When you run baxter enable, Baxter attempts to reset, but the motor relays fail to click ON, or click ON for half a second and immediately shut off with:

WARN (/realtime_loop,RobotStatePublisher.cpp:179) GlobalEnable requested, robot is still resetting
ERROR (/realtime_loop,Joint.cpp:337) Joint right_e0 tripped mechanical joint limit watchdog!

3. The Mechanics of the Twist Joints (e0 & w0)

Each Baxter arm has 7 degrees of freedom:

Unlike humans, who have continuous range of motion through ball joints, Baxter's arms alternate between Pitch joints (hinges) and Roll joints (rotary twists): - s0: Shoulder Yaw / Pan - s1: Shoulder Pitch (Arm up / down) - e0: Elbow Roll / Upper Arm Twist (Rotation along bicep axis, like turning a screwdriver) - e1: Elbow Pitch (Bends forearm toward upper arm) - w0: Wrist Roll / Forearm Twist (Rotation of forearm around its long axis) - w1: Wrist Pitch (Wrist flex up / down) - w2: Wrist Roll (End-effector plate rotation)

The Mechanical Limit Trap:

Joints e0 and w0 have a software limit of [-3.054, +3.054] radians ($\pm 175.0^\circ$). Just beyond $175^\circ$ lies a solid internal mechanical hard stop.

When Baxter is moved, stored, or manually jostled while powered OFF, the forearm or upper arm is frequently twisted all the way around until it jams firmly against the $-174^\circ$ or $+174^\circ$ hard stop. When Baxter boots, its internal position encoders read:

right_e0: -3.037 rad (-174.0°)  [LIMIT TRIPPED]
right_w0: -3.059 rad (-175.3°)  [LIMIT TRIPPED]
right_e1: -0.062 rad (  -3.6°)  [HYPEREXTENDED]

Because the joints are jammed against their limits, Baxter's internal safety watchdog considers the robot in imminent danger of mechanical damage and refuses to energize the motor bus relays.

4. Series Elastic Actuators (SEA) & Gravity Torques

In addition to joint limits, Baxter uses Series Elastic Actuators (SEAs). Inside every joint, a planar spring measures the torque between the motor and the limb.

  • If Baxter's arms are resting completely outstretched horizontally like planks, the cantilevered weight of the arm exerts up to $30\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ of pure gravity torque on the unpowered springs.
  • If the arm is hyperextended backwards (right_e1 at $-3.6^\circ$), the elbow joint cannot rotate forward under motor power without severe resistance.

5. The Physical Untwisting Procedure (Takes 10 Seconds)

To clear the error flags and allow the motors to enable, follow these manual steps:

               +----------------------------------------+
               |  STEP 1: BEND THE ELBOW (e1)           |
               |  Gently flex elbow forward to ~45°-90° |
               +-------------------+--------------------+
                                   │
                                   ▼
               +----------------------------------------+
               |  STEP 2: UNTWIST THE FOREARM (w0)      |
               |  Rotate cuff so buttons face UPWARD    |
               +-------------------+--------------------+
                                   │
                                   ▼
               +----------------------------------------+
               |  STEP 3: UNTWIST THE UPPER ARM (e0)    |
               |  Rotate bicep into neutral center      |
               +-------------------+--------------------+
                                   │
                                   ▼
               +----------------------------------------+
               |  STEP 4: LIFT SLIGHTLY & RUN RESET     |
               |  Stop and seek qualified assistance    |
               |  before powered recovery              |
               +----------------------------------------+

Step 1: Bend the Elbow (e1) Forward

Look at both arms. If an arm is locked completely straight or bent backwards: - Grab the forearm and bend the elbow forward into a relaxed L-shape ($45^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ angle). - This takes e1 from $-3.6^\circ$ up to $+45^\circ \dots +90^\circ$, cleanly clearing the hyperextension limit.

Step 2: Untwist the Forearm (w0)

  • Look at the end-effector cuff: the circular button and navigator dial should be facing naturally UPWARD or FORWARD, not twisted upside down or backwards against the stop.
  • Rotate the forearm about a quarter to half turn until the joint angle moves from $-174^\circ$ toward $0^\circ \dots +90^\circ$.

Step 3: Untwist the Upper Arm (e0)

  • Rotate the bicep / elbow joint so that the elbow hinge is pointing outward/backward naturally.

[!TIP] Using the Zero-G Cuff Squeeze Sensor: On each wrist cuff, there is a soft rubber squeeze ring. When Baxter's 12V logic power is ON, squeezing this rubber ring engages Zero-Gravity Mode, opening the motor brakes and allowing smooth, effortless manual positioning!

6. Live Joint Angle & Safety Monitoring Script

To observe the joint angles in real time while you untwist the arms, use this standalone monitoring utility:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Live Joint Twist & Safety Monitor for Baxter."""
import asyncio
from baxter import Baxter

async def monitor():
    async with Baxter() as robot:
        print("\n=== BAXTER ARM TWIST & SAFETY MONITOR ===")
        print("Watching right_e0, right_w0, right_e1. Rotate joints into safe range:\n")

        while True:
            state = await robot.get_state()
            joints = await robot.get_joint_positions()

            e0 = joints.get("right_e0", 0.0) * 180.0 / 3.14159
            w0 = joints.get("right_w0", 0.0) * 180.0 / 3.14159
            e1 = joints.get("right_e1", 0.0) * 180.0 / 3.14159

            e0_ok = -160.0 < e0 < 160.0
            w0_ok = -160.0 < w0 < 160.0
            e1_ok = e1 > 10.0

            st_e0 = "OK" if e0_ok else "JAMMED NEAR STOP"
            st_w0 = "OK" if w0_ok else "JAMMED NEAR STOP"
            st_e1 = "OK" if e1_ok else "HYPEREXTENDED"

            print(f"\r[STATUS] e0: {e0:6.1f}° ({st_e0:16}) | w0: {w0:6.1f}° ({st_w0:16}) | e1: {e1:5.1f}° ({st_e1:13}) | Error: {state.get('error')}", end="")

            # Monitor only. Do not automatically energize a robot during manual handling.
            await asyncio.sleep(0.5)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    asyncio.run(monitor())

2. Baxter's State Machine Architecture

Baxter's top-level power controller runs an embedded state machine that coordinates 48V motor power relays, watchdog heartbeats, Series Elastic Actuator amplifiers, and collision models:

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Disabled: System Boot / AC Power On
    Disabled --> Resetting: /robot/set_super_reset pulse
    Resetting --> Disabled: Faults Cleared
    Disabled --> Enabled: /robot/set_super_enable (True)
    Enabled --> Disabled: /robot/set_super_enable (False)
    Enabled --> Stopped: Hardware E-Stop / Software Stop
    Disabled --> Stopped: E-Stop Tripped
    Stopped --> Resetting: Release E-Stop + set_super_reset

State Flags in baxter_core_msgs/AssemblyState:

  • enabled: False, stopped: False, error: False: Robot is in standby logic mode. Relay open; arm motors unpowered. Ready to enable.
  • enabled: True, stopped: False, error: False: 48V relays closed; motor servo loops active at 1000 Hz. Robot is fully operational.
  • stopped: True: Emergency stop asserted (hardware switch or software stop). Motors cut immediately.
  • error: True: A hardware fault, joint limit violation, or amplifier overcurrent has occurred. Must be reset before enabling.

3. The Pulse Handshake: Why enable Alone Can Fail

If a joint has tripped a limit or an E-stop button was pressed and released, Baxter enters a latched fault state (error: True, estop_button: 3).

In this condition, simply commanding enable(True) is rejected by the controller:

WARN (/realtime_loop,RobotStatePublisher.cpp:179) GlobalEnable requested, robot is still resetting

The Required 3-Step Handshake:

To guarantee that the motor relays latch, you must execute the canonical reset sequence:

  1. Assert Global Disable: Publish data: False to /robot/set_super_enable (std_msgs/Bool). Wait 100 ms for motor controllers to de-energize.
  2. Pulse Global Reset: Publish an empty message to /robot/set_super_reset (std_msgs/Empty). Wait 300–500 ms for joint amplifiers to run power-on self tests (POST) and clear fault registers.
  3. Assert Global Enable: Publish data: True to /robot/set_super_enable (std_msgs/Bool) at 10 Hz until enabled == True is confirmed on /robot/state.

baxter-py automates this entire handshake internally when you run baxter enable or call await robot.enable().

5. Tucking and Untucking Baxter's Arms

When Baxter is shipped or packed away, its arms are folded tightly into its chest in the "Shipping Pose" (Tucked).

The Collision Avoidance Paradox:

In normal operation, Baxter runs an internal geometric collision model that actively stops the arms from colliding with each other or the torso. However, in the Shipping Pose, the arms are folded within millimeters of the torso. If collision avoidance remained active, the robot would lock up and refuse to move out of the tucked pose!

How baxter-py Solves This:

During baxter untuck and baxter tuck, the controller temporarily publishes heartbeat pulses to: - /robot/limb/left/suppress_collision_avoidance - /robot/limb/right/suppress_collision_avoidance

This allows the trajectory generator to unfold the arms safely along a deterministic trajectory without triggering collision halts.

2. Series Elastic Actuators (SEA) & The Resonance Trap

Traditional industrial robotic arms (like KUKA or FANUC) have rigid harmonic drives and stiff steel shafts. You can command them with instantaneous step inputs, and the arm will snap to position aggressively.

Baxter is fundamentally different: every joint has a calibrated torsional spring between the motor and the limb.

  [Motor + Gearbox] ─── (Torsion Spring: Ks) ─── [Output Link / Arm]

The Spring Resonance Phenomenon:

If you publish a sudden step change in target position: 1. The motor accelerates rapidly, winding up the torsion spring. 2. The spring releases stored energy, overshooting the target. 3. The limb violently oscillates back and forth at the mechanical natural frequency ($\approx 2 \dots 5\text{ Hz}$), causing Baxter to shudder and trip an amplifier torque safety error!

The Mathematical Solution: Trajectory Filtering

All commanded trajectories must be smoothly interpolated. baxter-py employs a digital first-order low-pass filter and minimum-jerk (quintic polynomial) interpolation:

$$q_k = q_{k-1} + \alpha \cdot (q_{\text{target}} - q_{k-1})$$

Where $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ is the smoothing factor calibrated to Baxter's internal SEA time constants ($\tau \approx 0.1\text{ s}$).

5. Python API: Multi-Joint Trajectory Interpolation

Here is how to create a custom smooth multi-waypoint motion generator using baxter-py:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Lab 4: Custom Smooth Arm Motion Generator."""
import asyncio
import math
from baxter import Baxter

async def move_to_pose(robot, arm_name, target_joints, duration=2.5, steps=50):
    """Interpolates joints smoothly using minimum-jerk cosine ramping."""
    current_joints = await robot.get_joint_positions()
    arm_joints = [j for j in current_joints if j.startswith(arm_name)]

    start_angles = {j: current_joints[j] for j in arm_joints}
    dt = duration / steps

    for i in range(steps + 1):
        # Cosine s-curve profile (0.0 to 1.0)
        t = i / steps
        alpha = 0.5 * (1.0 - math.cos(math.pi * t))

        step_command = {}
        for j, target_val in target_joints.items():
            start_val = start_angles.get(j, 0.0)
            step_command[j] = start_val + alpha * (target_val - start_val)

        await robot.send_joint_command(arm_name, step_command)
        await asyncio.sleep(dt)

async def main():
    async with Baxter() as robot:
        state = await robot.get_state()
        if not state.get("enabled"):
            await robot.enable()

        print("Executing custom waving choreography...")

        # High wave pose
        wave_high = {
            "right_s0": -0.20,
            "right_s1": -0.80,
            "right_e0": 1.20,
            "right_e1": 1.70,
            "right_w0": 0.0,
            "right_w1": -0.60,
            "right_w2": 0.0,
        }

        # Wave left pose
        wave_tilt = {
            "right_s0": -0.20,
            "right_s1": -0.80,
            "right_e0": 1.20,
            "right_e1": 1.70,
            "right_w0": 0.0,
            "right_w1": 0.20,
            "right_w2": 0.0,
        }

        await move_to_pose(robot, "right", wave_high, duration=2.0)

        # Oscillate wrist for 3 cycles
        for _ in range(3):
            await move_to_pose(robot, "right", wave_tilt, duration=0.6)
            await move_to_pose(robot, "right", wave_high, duration=0.6)

        # Return to neutral
        await robot.untuck(duration=2.5)
        print("Motion complete!")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    asyncio.run(main())

6. Python OpenCV Computer Vision Pipeline

Here is a complete, runnable Python script that subscribes to the head camera stream, decodes frames into NumPy arrays, and runs real-time edge detection:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Lab 6: OpenCV Real-Time Vision Processing on Baxter Camera."""
import asyncio
import cv2
import numpy as np
from baxter import Baxter

async def process_stream():
    async with Baxter() as robot:
        print("Subscribing to Baxter head_camera stream...")

        async for frame_bgr in robot.camera.stream("head_camera", resolution=(640, 400)):
            # 1. Convert to Grayscale
            gray = cv2.cvtColor(frame_bgr, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)

            # 2. Gaussian Blur to reduce high-frequency noise
            blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (5, 5), 0)

            # 3. Canny Edge Detection
            edges = cv2.Canny(blurred, 50, 150)

            # 4. Find Contours
            contours, _ = cv2.findContours(edges, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)

            # 5. Draw bounding boxes around large objects
            annotated = frame_bgr.copy()
            for cnt in contours:
                if cv2.contourArea(cnt) > 500:
                    x, y, w, h = cv2.boundingRect(cnt)
                    cv2.rectangle(annotated, (x, y), (x + w, y + h), (0, 255, 0), 2)
                    cv2.putText(annotated, "OBJECT", (x, y - 5), cv2.FONT_HERSHEY_SIMPLEX, 0.5, (0, 255, 0), 1)

            # Display side-by-side
            cv2.imshow("Baxter Head Camera - Raw vs Edges", np.hstack([annotated, cv2.cvtColor(edges, cv2.COLOR_GRAY2BGR)]))

            # Press 'q' to exit
            if cv2.waitKey(1) & 0xFF == ord('q'):
                break

    cv2.destroyAllWindows()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    asyncio.run(process_stream())

3. Mathematical Foundations: Monocular 3D Metric Reconstruction

Extracting 3D metric coordinates $(X, Y, Z)$ from a single 2D camera without LiDAR or depth sensors is an ill-posed inverse problem. We solve it by exploiting known human anatomical proportions.

A. Estimating Depth ($Z$):

The human metacarpophalangeal (MCP) knuckle span (distance between index MCP and pinky MCP) is remarkably consistent across adult humans: $$W_{\text{phys}} \approx 0.085\text{ meters } (8.5\text{ cm})$$

Using the pinhole camera projection model: $$w_{\text{pixel}} = f \cdot \frac{W_{\text{phys}}}{Z}$$

Where $f$ is the focal length in pixels. We can solve directly for the metric depth $Z$: $$Z = \frac{f \cdot W_{\text{phys}}}{w_{\text{pixel}}}$$

B. Estimating Lateral Position ($X$) and Height ($Y$):

Once metric depth $Z$ is known: $$X = \frac{(u - c_x) \cdot Z}{f}, \quad Y = \frac{(v - c_y) \cdot Z}{f}$$

Where $(u, v)$ is the pixel position of the index fingertip, and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the camera optical center.

4. The Clutch Mechanism (Pinch-to-Engage)

In industrial teleoperation, you need a way to reposition your hand without moving the robot (like lifting an optical mouse off a mousepad).

baxter track implements a Pinch Clutch: - Disengaged (Clutch Open): Move your hand into position. The HUD displays CLUTCH: DISENGAGED (STANDBY). Baxter maintains its current hold pose. - Engaged (Clutch Closed): Pinch your index finger and thumb together (or press [e] to lock continuous tracking). The HUD turns green (CLUTCH: ENGAGED), and Baxter's arm instantly tracks your fingertip in 1:1 metric space!

The teleoperator runs an asynchronous processing pipeline: 1. Camera Ingestion (60 FPS): Captures frames from webcam or Baxter head camera. 2. Over weeks of continuous operation, thermal expansion and mechanical strain cause minor drift in the analog torque sensors and position encoders of Baxter's Series Elastic Actuators (SEAs). If left uncalibrated, the arms may slowly droop or feel "heavy" in Zero-G mode.

A. The Tare Routine (Weekly Maintenance)

A Tare measures the zero-torque resting bias of each joint spring under known gravity configurations. - Frequency: Approximately once per week with regular laboratory use. - Duration: ~2 minutes. - Procedure: Ensure the arms are completely unloaded (no heavy custom tools attached). Command Baxter to execute a tare cycle: bash # Via baxter-py or ROS command baxter reset baxter untuck

B. Full Arm Sensor Calibration (Monthly Maintenance)

If joint angles report offsets exceeding $2^\circ$ or motors feel jerky: - Frequency: Monthly. - Runs a multi-pose kinematic self-test across all 14 arm joints to recalibrate spring stiffness parameters and encoder offsets.
